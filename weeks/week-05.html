02 · Core concept

What is vulnerability analysis?

Vulnerability analysis is the process of identifying, understanding, validating, and prioritizing weaknesses that could affect the confidentiality, integrity, or availability of systems and information. It helps an organization decide which security issues require attention first.

Vulnerability assessment vs. penetration testing

Vulnerability assessmentPenetration testing
Identifies and classifies potential weaknesses across a defined environment.Tests whether selected weaknesses can be exploited within an approved scope.
Usually emphasizes coverage, risk ranking, and remediation.Usually emphasizes validated impact, attack paths, and security control effectiveness.
Often uses automated scanners plus manual validation.Requires additional manual testing and carefully controlled validation.

Neither activity replaces the other. An organization may use regular vulnerability assessments to maintain visibility and periodic penetration tests to evaluate selected risks in greater depth.

Important distinction: A scanner finding is not automatically a confirmed vulnerability. Results must be reviewed against the affected asset, software version, configuration, and available evidence.
03 · Vulnerability intelligence

Where vulnerability information comes from

  • CVE (Common Vulnerabilities and Exposures): A standardized identifier assigned to a publicly disclosed cybersecurity vulnerability.
  • NVD (National Vulnerability Database): A U.S. government resource that enriches vulnerability records with analysis, references, and scoring information.
  • Vendor security advisories: Official notices from software and hardware vendors describing affected products and fixes.
  • Security researcher disclosures: Technical reports that may provide details about a weakness and its impact.
  • Configuration and asset inventories: Internal records that help determine whether an organization actually uses an affected product or configuration.

Useful reference sites:

  • CVE Program
  • National Vulnerability Database
  • CISA Known Exploited Vulnerabilities Catalog

Always check the vendor advisory and current asset details. A CVE may affect only specific product versions, configurations, or deployment conditions.

04 · Risk scoring

Common Vulnerability Scoring System (CVSS)

CVSS is an open framework for describing the technical severity of vulnerabilities. The CVSS base score ranges from 0.0 to 10.0. It helps communicate severity, but it is not a complete measurement of business risk.

CVSS v3.1 scoreSeverityGeneral meaning
0.0NoneNo severity score assigned by the scale.
0.1–3.9LowLimited technical impact or exploit conditions.
4.0–6.9MediumModerate technical severity.
7.0–8.9HighSignificant technical severity.
9.0–10.0CriticalVery high technical severity.

CVSS v3.1 base metrics

MetricWhat it measuresExamples of values
Attack Vector (AV)How an attacker reaches the vulnerable component.Network, Adjacent, Local, Physical
Attack Complexity (AC)Conditions beyond the attacker's control needed for exploitation.Low, High
Privileges Required (PR)Privileges needed before exploitation.None, Low, High
User Interaction (UI)Whether another user must take an action.None, Required
Scope (S)Whether impact extends beyond the vulnerable component's security authority.Unchanged, Changed
Confidentiality (C)Impact on information confidentiality.None, Low, High
Integrity (I)Impact on information accuracy and trustworthiness.None, Low, High
Availability (A)Impact on access to systems or information.None, Low, High
CVSS v3.1 is covered here because it is part of the course material. CVSS v4.0 is also available. Always state which CVSS version a score uses and consult the official specification when calculating or interpreting scores.

CVSS severity is not the same as business priority

A high CVSS score deserves attention, but remediation priority should also consider whether the asset is internet-facing, whether exploitation is known to occur, the value of the affected data, existing mitigations, and the organization's risk tolerance.

05 · Finding classification

Understanding vulnerability findings

  • Misconfiguration: A setting that weakens security, such as an unnecessary exposed service or overly broad permission.
  • Outdated software: A product version that no longer receives security updates or contains a known weakness.
  • Missing security patch: A vendor fix has not been applied to an affected asset.
  • Weak authentication: Authentication controls do not adequately protect access.
  • Excessive privilege: An account or service has more permissions than it needs.
  • Information disclosure: A system reveals information that should not be publicly accessible.

False positives and false negatives

TermMeaningWhy it matters
False positiveA tool reports a vulnerability that is not actually present or applicable.Can waste time and lead to unnecessary remediation.
False negativeA real vulnerability exists but the assessment fails to identify it.Can leave an organization exposed to risk.

Validation can include checking the installed software version, vendor advisory, configuration, patch history, and scanner evidence. Do not prove a finding by exploiting a production system unless that activity is explicitly authorized and safely planned.

Scanner limitations

  • Scanners may not understand the full business context of an asset.
  • Version detection can be incomplete or inaccurate.
  • Authenticated scans may reveal configuration issues that unauthenticated scans miss.
  • Network filtering and unavailable services can limit visibility.
  • New vulnerabilities may not yet be represented in scanner checks.
  • Automated findings require human review and prioritization.
07 · Vulnerability management

The vulnerability management lifecycle

  1. Define scope: Identify approved assets, testing windows, methods, and exclusions.
  2. Discover assets: Maintain an accurate inventory of systems, software, and owners.
  3. Identify weaknesses: Use appropriate scanners, configuration reviews, and trusted advisories.
  4. Validate findings: Check evidence and determine whether each issue applies.
  5. Prioritize risk: Consider severity, exposure, known exploitation, business impact, and available mitigations.
  6. Remediate: Apply patches, change configurations, remove unnecessary services, or implement compensating controls.
  7. Retest: Confirm that the fix is effective and the finding is resolved.
  8. Report and improve: Record results, owners, deadlines, exceptions, and lessons learned.
08 · Professional reporting

How to write a vulnerability report

A useful vulnerability report helps technical teams understand what was found, why it matters, and how to fix it. Each finding should be evidence-based and reproducible within the approved scope.

Report fieldWhat to include
Finding titleShort, clear description of the weakness.
AssetAuthorized hostname, asset identifier, and affected service.
EvidenceRelevant scanner output, version information, and safe supporting screenshots.
ReferenceCVE or vendor advisory, where applicable.
SeverityCVSS score, version, and severity rating, if available.
ImpactPotential confidentiality, integrity, availability, or operational consequences.
RecommendationSpecific patch, configuration change, access restriction, or compensating control.
StatusOpen, in progress, mitigated, fixed, accepted, or retest required.
09 · Practical lab 5

Vulnerability scanning and analysis

Objectives

  • Understand how a vulnerability scanner works.
  • Run a controlled assessment against Metasploitable in your isolated lab.
  • Review vulnerability descriptions and CVSS scores.
  • Recognize possible false positives and scanner limitations.
  • Prepare a prioritized remediation report.
Lab safety: Use only your own Metasploitable VM on a private host-only or dedicated isolated virtual network. Confirm its IP address from the VM console. Do not scan public IP addresses, campus networks, or other devices. Use a conservative scan profile and avoid denial-of-service or exploit checks.

Step 1: Prepare the lab

  1. Confirm network isolation.

    Verify both virtual machines use the same isolated network and that Metasploitable is not using bridged networking. Take a clean snapshot before scanning.

  2. Identify the lab target.

    Read the IP address from the Metasploitable console. In Kali, inspect the network configuration:

    ip addr

    Use only the address belonging to your own target VM.

Step 2: Use a vulnerability scanner

Choose one scanner that your computer can run reliably. Nessus and Greenbone can require significant memory and disk space; use a separate machine or an appropriately resourced lab if needed.

  1. Nessus: Review the official Nessus product page for current editions, eligibility, licensing, and installation instructions. Install it only from Tenable's official distribution. The local interface commonly uses https://localhost:8834.
  2. Configure a conservative scan: Create a basic network assessment using the lab target IP only. Leave credentials blank for an unauthenticated scan. Disable denial-of-service, destructive, and exploit-oriented checks. Review the scan settings before launching.
  3. Alternative: Review the official Greenbone documentation if you are using the Greenbone Community Edition. Follow its installation guidance and choose a safe assessment profile for the isolated target.

Step 3: Review results

  1. Sort findings by severity and review the evidence for each result.
  2. Record the finding name, affected service, port, CVE reference if present, CVSS score, and scanner recommendation.
  3. Check whether the affected software or configuration is actually present on the lab target.
  4. Mark uncertain results as requiring validation; do not label them confirmed without evidence.
  5. Do not run exploit code or intrusive verification checks as part of this exercise.

Step 4: Optional web server review

If your isolated Metasploitable VM exposes a web server and your lab resources permit it, you may use Nikto for a basic, non-destructive configuration review against that lab web service only:

nikto -h http://<LAB_TARGET_IP> -output nikto-results.txt

Replace the placeholder only with the confirmed address of your own isolated lab target. Nikto can send numerous requests; do not run it against production or third-party websites.

Step 5: Prioritize and report

Create a table of the most important findings. Include severity, asset importance, exposure, evidence confidence, remediation, and retest status. Explain why the highest CVSS score is not necessarily the organization's highest business priority.

Expected outcomes

  • Completed scanner assessment of the isolated lab target.
  • Prioritized findings with CVSS scores where available.
  • At least one example of a result requiring validation.
  • Remediation recommendations for selected findings.
  • A professional vulnerability assessment report.
10 · Assessment

Assignment 5

Part A: Vulnerability report (70 points)

Using only your isolated Metasploitable lab target, prepare a vulnerability assessment report. If fewer than 10 findings are returned, report the actual number; never invent results.

  1. Identify and document up to 10 important findings from your scan.
  2. For each finding, include its title, affected service or port, CVE reference if available, CVSS score and version, and severity.
  3. Explain the potential impact in clear language.
  4. Provide a specific remediation recommendation supported by a vendor advisory or trusted reference where possible.
  5. Explain how you validated the result or why it remains unconfirmed.

Part B: Analysis questions (30 points)

  1. What is the difference between a false positive and a false negative?
  2. Why might an unauthenticated scan miss configuration or patch-related vulnerabilities?
  3. How does CVSS help teams communicate technical severity?
  4. Why should business context and known exploitation influence remediation priority?
  5. Identify three limitations of automated vulnerability scanners.
Submission format: Professional PDF report, approximately 5–10 pages. Include the scope, lab setup, scan methodology, selected evidence, prioritization, remediation plan, and references. Include only data from your authorized lab.
