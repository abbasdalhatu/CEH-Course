02 · Core concept

What is footprinting?

Footprinting is the structured process of collecting information about an organization, domain, or system to understand its public-facing digital presence. In an authorized security assessment, this helps define what is known about the target before any technical testing begins.

Information that may be collected

  • Public domain names and published IP address ranges.
  • Publicly documented services and technologies.
  • Organization-level contact channels and published email formats.
  • Public subsidiaries, business units, and locations.
  • Technology clues in official documentation, job advertisements, and press releases.
  • Public DNS records and historical website information.
Data minimization: Collect only information relevant to the approved assessment. Avoid compiling personal profiles, collecting private contact details, or retaining information that is not needed.
03 · Reconnaissance methods

Passive vs. active reconnaissance

MethodMeaningExamples
PassiveUses information already published or available through third-party sources without directly probing the target's systems.Official websites, public reports, search engines, published DNS data, public registries.
ActiveInvolves sending queries or requests directly to target infrastructure. It may be logged, affect services, or require explicit authorization.Direct DNS queries, service discovery, port scanning, and network mapping within an approved scope.

Passive does not automatically mean risk-free or unrestricted. Respect privacy, site terms, applicable law, and the purpose for which information was published. Active reconnaissance must remain inside the written scope.

04 · Intelligence gathering

Open Source Intelligence (OSINT)

OSINT is the practice of collecting, validating, and analyzing information from publicly available sources. Security teams use it to understand an organization's public exposure and to make informed defensive decisions.

Common public sources

  • Official company websites, documentation, and press releases.
  • Public domain-registration records, where available.
  • Public DNS records.
  • Search engines and public archives such as the Internet Archive Wayback Machine.
  • Public technology documentation and job advertisements.
  • Official social media pages and public organizational announcements.

OSINT workflow

  1. Define the organization and the approved research question.
  2. Identify relevant public sources.
  3. Collect only relevant information and record its source and date.
  4. Cross-check important findings with another reliable source.
  5. Separate verified facts from assumptions.
  6. Summarize defensive implications and securely store the report.
05 · Search techniques

Search engine operators

Search operators refine ordinary search queries. They can help locate public pages on a domain you own or are authorized to assess. The examples below use example.com, a reserved example domain.

OperatorPurposeSafe example
site:Limits results to a domain.site:example.com
filetype:Looks for a particular public file type.site:example.com filetype:pdf
intitle:Looks for words in page titles.site:example.com intitle:about
inurl:Looks for words in page URLs.site:example.com inurl:news
"phrase"Searches for an exact phrase.site:example.com "contact us"
Do not use search operators to seek exposed credentials, private records, database dumps, or restricted administrative material. If sensitive information appears unexpectedly, do not open or download it; record the minimum necessary evidence and report it through an authorized channel.
06 · Domain intelligence

DNS reconnaissance

The Domain Name System (DNS) translates human-readable domain names into information that computers use to locate services. Public DNS records can reveal how a domain publishes its web, mail, and other services.

RecordPurpose
AMaps a hostname to an IPv4 address.
AAAAMaps a hostname to an IPv6 address.
MXIdentifies mail servers for a domain.
NSIdentifies authoritative name servers.
TXTStores text data, often used for domain verification and email-security policies.
CNAMECreates an alias from one hostname to another.

Useful commands

These examples query the reserved example.com domain for basic DNS learning:

nslookup example.com
nslookup -type=MX example.com
nslookup -type=NS example.com

dig example.com A
dig example.com MX
dig example.com NS

Use direct DNS enumeration or subdomain discovery against a real organization only when its written authorization explicitly permits it. Do not assume that a public domain or a bug-bounty listing authorizes every technique.

08 · Practical lab 2

Passive reconnaissance and DNS research

Objectives

  • Understand the difference between passive and active reconnaissance.
  • Collect basic public information about a reserved example domain.
  • Query common DNS record types.
  • Practice safe search operators.
  • Prepare a concise, source-backed reconnaissance report.
Lab scope: Use example.com for the command examples. For any real organization, limit the exercise to passive public research unless you have explicit written permission covering the specific active technique.
  1. Review domain registration information.

    If WHOIS is installed, run:

    whois example.com

    Record only general domain-registration details that are publicly returned. Some fields may be hidden for privacy.

  2. Query DNS records.

    Run the following commands and note what each record type tells you:

    nslookup example.com
nslookup -type=MX example.com
nslookup -type=NS example.com
dig example.com A
dig example.com MX
dig example.com TXT
  3. Practice search operators.

    Enter these safe example queries into a search engine:

    site:example.com
site:example.com filetype:pdf
site:example.com intitle:about
site:example.com "contact us"

    Explain how each operator changes the search results. Do not search for credentials, private files, or exposed databases.

  4. Review public website technologies.

    Use the Wappalyzer website to learn how technology identification works. For hands-on inspection of a real site, use a website you own or have permission to assess.

  5. Organize your findings.

    Create a table with the source, observation, date collected, confidence level, and defensive relevance. Clearly label anything that has not been independently verified.

Expected outcomes

  • Explanation of passive and active reconnaissance.
  • Basic WHOIS and DNS observations for the example domain.
  • Five correctly explained search operators or queries.
  • A short, organized report with sources and dates.
09 · Assessment

Assignment 2

Part A: Reconnaissance report (60 points)

Choose an organization with a published vulnerability disclosure or bug-bounty policy. Conduct passive public-source research only, and follow the program's published scope and rules. Do not scan, probe, log in, test vulnerabilities, or contact employees.

  1. Summarize the organization's public digital footprint using official sources.
  2. List up to 10 publicly documented subdomains only when they are clearly published by official sources or passive datasets permitted by the program.
  3. Describe technologies identified from public documentation or the organization's own website.
  4. Summarize organization-level information from official public pages; do not compile personal profiles or private contact details.
  5. Explain two defensive observations and support each with a source.

Part B: Search operator challenge (40 points)

  1. Create 10 search queries using operators such as site:, filetype:, intitle:, inurl:, and exact-phrase search.
  2. Test them on example.com or a domain you own.
  3. Explain what each query is designed to find and what its results actually show.
  4. Do not search for or access exposed credentials, private records, or restricted files.
Submission format: Professional PDF report, 5–8 pages. Include your research scope, sources, dates, findings, limitations, and defensive recommendations. Remove unnecessary personal information before submission.
